Train the GAAP

Your bookkeeper is the most unsupported person in your business.

In a corporation, a staff accountant has a senior, a controller and a CFO to turn to. In a small business, the bookkeeper usually learned on the job, taught themselves QuickBooks, and has no one to ask. Train the GAAP gives them the why behind the work, and someone in their corner.

In a small business
  1. OwnerBusy running everything else

Many small-business bookkeepers never went to accounting school. They worked beside someone, learned the software and picked up the rest on the job. They're good at it. They've just never had anyone explain why it works the way it does.

Closing the loop

Most of it makes sense the moment someone explains it.

Why do we follow this protocol? Why did that get reclassed? Why did the CPA change my entries at year end? These aren't hard questions. They're questions nobody ever answered.

  • Why certain protocols exist, and what breaks when they're skipped
  • Why the CPA's year-end adjustments happened, so they don't repeat
  • Why things get changed, and how to change them the right way
  • The core GAAP principles behind everyday entries

What owners assume

The CPA isn't the backup you think it is.

Owners often assume their bookkeeper can just call the CPA. But a CPA knows the general ledger and journal entries. Your bookkeeper knows your system: how an entry actually goes through QuickBooks, how it's set up, where things land.

What's missing is someone who knows both, accounting and systems. Someone your bookkeeper, and anyone else working in the books, can call, bounce things off, and ask the question they've been afraid to ask.

An ally, not an auditor

Someone in their corner.

No judgment for a mistake. No pushback for asking. Just someone with 20+ years in accounting and systems who understands their world, because I've worked in it.

  • A call when something doesn't look right
  • A second set of eyes before an entry goes through
  • Plain answers about why, not just what
  • Credit for the work they've been carrying alone

The core of GAAP

The why behind the work: five principles.

These are the foundations most often named as the heart of financial reporting. Once your bookkeeper knows them, the protocols and the CPA's changes start to make sense.

Principle 1

Revenue Recognition

Record revenue when it's earned, usually when the goods or services are delivered, not when the cash shows up.

ExampleYou finish a repair job in March and the client pays in April. The income belongs in March.

When it's missedMarch looks weak, April looks great, and neither month tells you the truth.

Principle 2

Matching (Expense Recognition)

Record expenses in the same period as the revenue they helped earn.

ExampleA store buys holiday inventory in October and sells it in December. The cost of that inventory is an expense in December, alongside the sales.

When it's missedOctober shows a loss, December shows a fat margin, and your real profit per sale is invisible.

Principle 3

Historical Cost

Record assets at what you paid for them, no matter how their market value moves later.

ExampleA company buys an office building for $500,000. It stays at $500,000 on the books even if real estate values skyrocket.

When it's missedThe balance sheet turns into opinion, and no lender or buyer will trust it.

Principle 4

Full Disclosure

Include every material fact a reader needs, with notes that explain it, so lenders and investors aren't misled.

ExampleIf the business faces a lawsuit that could threaten its finances, that risk goes in the notes.

When it's missedIt surfaces in due diligence instead, and the buyer starts wondering what else wasn't said.

Principle 5

Consistency

Use the same methods from one period to the next, so reports can be compared over time.

ExampleYou can't switch your inventory method every quarter without formally disclosing and explaining the change.

When it's missedThis year can't be compared to last year, and every trend line on your dashboard is suspect.

The point

Books a buyer can trust.

Lenders, CPAs and buyers read your books through these five principles. A team that understands them builds books that pass that test the first time.
